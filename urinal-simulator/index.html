How the physics works

  1. The stream is a projectile

    It leaves the nozzle at about half your height, at your chosen velocity and aim, and follows a ballistic arc. Over less than a metre, air drag on a 3.2 mm jet is negligible. Flow rate is Q = v·πd²/4, so 2.8 m/s delivers about 22 mL/s.

  2. It breaks into drops

    Surface tension makes any jet unstable (Rayleigh–Plateau). Disturbances grow at ω = 0.34·√(σ/ρa³), so the stream turns into a train of 6 mm drops after about 64 ms, roughly 15–20 cm from you at typical speeds. A jet that lands before that point splashes far less.

  3. The impact decides the splash

    Drops splash when K = Wen½·Ren¼ exceeds about 58, using only the velocity normal to the surface. A steep hit on a vertical wall puts nearly all of the speed into that normal component. A glancing hit below about 20–30° barely splashes, which is the idea behind low-angle fixtures.

  4. Every droplet is flown home

    The splashed volume is released as 6,000 parcels of sub-millimetre droplets per urinal, each with its own size, speed and direction. They fly under gravity and Reynolds-dependent drag until they hit you, the fixture, the partition, the wall or the floor. One parcel in four belongs to the prompt-splash fringe: tiny droplets thrown faster than the impact itself. Only those are fast enough to climb 30 cm and reach your hands, which is why hands get hit only by steep, fast impacts.

  • Pan, Hood et al., University of Waterloo (2022, PNAS Nexus): the critical-angle argument and the low-angle "Nautilus" fixture that the Nautilus option is modelled on.
  • Thoroddsen (2002), J. Fluid Mech.: the ejecta sheet, whose droplets leave faster than the impacting drop.
  • Range & Feuillebois (1998), J. Colloid Interface Sci.: surface roughness lowers the splash threshold, which is why a urinal cake splashes more than glazed porcelain.
  • Mundo, Sommerfeld & Tropea (1995), Int. J. Multiphase Flow: the K splashing threshold for drop–wall impacts.
  • Lord Rayleigh (1878), On the instability of jets; Drillis & Contini (1966) for the body proportions.
